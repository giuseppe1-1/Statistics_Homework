1The four modes of convergence

Let X1, X2, … and X be random variables on a probability space (Ω, ℱ, P): each Xn is a function of the outcome ω. “Xn → X” can mean four different things, depending on what has to be small.

ModeDefinitionIn words
In probability
Xn →P X		for every ε > 0: P(|Xn − X| > ε) → 0At a fixed large n the probability of a noticeable error is small. The “bad” outcomes may be different for every n.
Almost surely
Xn →a.s. X		P(ω : Xn(ω) → X(ω)) = 1  ⇔  for every ε: P(supk≥n |Xk − X| > ε) → 0For almost every outcome, the whole path Xn(ω) ends up (and stays) close to X(ω). The error has to be small simultaneously for all k ≥ n.
Mean square (L²)
Xn →L² X		E|Xn − X|² → 0The average squared error vanishes. Needs finite second moments; it is the mean squared error of an estimator.
In distribution
Xn →d X		Fn(x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of F  ⇔  E g(Xn) → E g(X) for every bounded continuous gOnly the laws become close. Xn need not be close to anything as a function of ω (they may even live on different probability spaces).

How they are related

Why the implications hold

L² ⇒ P :   P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E|Xn − X|² / ε²Markov / Chebyshev inequality a.s. ⇒ P :   P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ P(supk≥n |Xk − X| > ε) → 0the events {supk≥n … > ε} decrease to the null event {Xn ↛ X} P ⇒ d :   |E g(Xn) − E g(X)| ≤ E |g(Xn) − g(X)| → 0for bounded uniformly continuous g (and these g are enough to characterise convergence in distribution) Borel–Cantelli :   ∑n P(|Xn − X| > ε) < ∞ ⇒ Xn →a.s. Xa sufficient condition for a.s. convergence; the second lemma (independent events, divergent sum) shows when it fails
What is lost in each step. Moving from a.s. to P we forget which outcomes are bad and keep only how many; from P to d we forget the pairing between Xn and X and keep only the laws; L² is a different type of control (moments, not paths): it forbids rare but huge values, which a.s. and P allow.

2Counterexamples: five sequences, three modes measured

For each sequence Xn → 0 we estimate by simulation (M independent paths up to N) the three quantities that define the modes: P(|Xn| > ε) (in probability), P(supk≥n |Xk| > ε) (almost sure: it tends to 0 exactly when Xn → 0 a.s.), and E Xn² (L²). The sup is computed over k ≤ N, so for sequences that do not converge a.s. the orange curve has to fall near n = N: this is a truncation effect, not convergence.

3The Law of Large Numbers

X1, X2, … i.i.d. with mean μ; sample mean X̄n = (X1 + … + Xn)/n.

VersionStatementModeRole
Mean-square LLNif σ² = Var X < ∞: E(X̄n − μ)² = σ²/n → 0L²Exact rate of the MSE; direct from the additivity of the variance (Section 5).
Weak LLN (Chebyshev, Khinchin)E|X| < ∞ ⇒ X̄n →P μ. With a variance: P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²)in probabilityConsistency of the sample mean; the form needed for estimators.
Strong LLN (Kolmogorov)E|X| < ∞ ⇔ X̄n →a.s. μalmost sureStatement about a single infinite run: Monte Carlo and relative frequencies converge along the sequence you actually observe.

4The Central Limit Theorem

Zn = √n (X̄n − μ)/σ = (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n)  →d  N(0, 1)Xi i.i.d., 0 < σ² < ∞ (Lindeberg–Lévy). Equivalently P(Zn ≤ z) → Φ(z) for every z.
  • The mode is convergence in distribution, and nothing stronger is possible. Zn does not converge in probability (or a.s., or in L²) to anything: Zn and Z2n share half of their terms, correlation 1/√2, so Z2n − Zn has variance 2 − √2 for all large n and does not shrink (table below). Only the law of Zn settles down.
  • The LLN says where X̄n goes; the CLT says how it fluctuates around it: X̄n ≈ μ + σZ/√n. The LLN is the first-order result (a constant, via P / a.s.), the CLT the second-order result (a random fluctuation, via d).
  • Berry–Esseen quantifies the speed: supz |P(Zn ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C ρ/(σ³√n) with ρ = E|X − μ|³ and C ≤ 0.4748: the error is O(1/√n), and larger for skewed laws.
  • Slutsky (d combined with P) lets us replace σ by the sample standard deviation sn →P σ: √n(X̄n − μ)/sn →d N(0, 1), which is the basis of confidence intervals and z / t tests.

Zn does not converge in probability

5Why √n? Additivity of the variance and the role of independence

5.1 The calculation

Var(Sn) = ∑i Var(Xi) + 2 ∑i<j Cov(Xi, Xj)always true (bilinearity of the covariance) independent (or just uncorrelated): Cov(Xi, Xj) = 0 ⇒ Var(Sn) = nσ²the variance is additive; the standard deviation grows like √n, not like n sd(Sn) = σ√n,   sd(X̄n) = σ/√nfluctuations of the sum are of order √n, those of the mean of order 1/√n

The fluctuations of independent terms partly cancel (some are positive, some negative): n terms of size σ add up to σ√n, not to nσ. To see a non-degenerate limit one has to rescale by exactly this factor: na(X̄n − μ) has standard deviation σ na−1/2, which tends to 0 if a < 1/2 (this is the LLN: degenerate limit), to ∞ if a > 1/2, and stays at σ only for a = 1/2 (the CLT).

5.2 Without independence

The √n scaling relies on the correlations adding up to o(n). Below, n·Var(X̄n)/σ² for unit-variance Gaussian sequences (it is 1 for independent data):

Three regimes.
  • Independent: ratio = 1 for every n. Variance of the mean σ²/n.
  • Short memory (AR(1), correlations decay geometrically): the ratio converges to the long-run variance (1+φ)/(1−φ). The √n scaling and the CLT survive, with a larger constant: the effective sample size is n(1−φ)/(1+φ).
  • Long memory / common factor (equicorrelated ρ > 0): Var(X̄n) = σ²[1/n + (1 − 1/n)ρ] → ρσ² > 0. The mean does not even converge to μ (it converges to a random variable): the LLN, and with it the √n scaling, fails.

5.3 In statistical inference

  • Standard error. SE(X̄) = σ/√n, estimated by s/√n: to halve the error you need four times as much data. The sample size for a margin of error e at confidence 95% is n = (1.96 σ / e)².
  • √n-consistency and asymptotic normality. Most estimators θ̂n satisfy √n(θ̂n − θ) →d N(0, V): for the maximum-likelihood estimator V = 1/I(θ) (inverse Fisher information); by the delta method, √n(g(θ̂n) − g(θ)) →d N(0, g′(θ)² V). The requirement of independence (or weak dependence) is built into the proofs.
  • Test statistics such as √n(X̄ − μ0)/s are of order 1 under H0 and of order √n under an alternative: this is why power grows with n.

In one sentence. a.s. and P describe that the sample mean converges to μ (a.s. along every path, P at a fixed n); L² says how fast the error shrinks on average (σ/√n); convergence in distribution says what the remaining error looks like (Gaussian of size σ/√n), and the √n comes from the additivity of the variance of independent terms.
