1Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),    P(B) > 0Probability of A when we know that B has occurred: the sample space shrinks from Ω to B and probabilities are renormalised.
  • In probability theory it gives the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B), the law of total probability P(A) = ∑i P(A | Bi) P(Bi) (Bi a partition), and the definition of independence: A and B are independent iff P(A | B) = P(A). It extends to random variables: the conditional distribution f(y | x) = f(x, y)/fX(x) and the conditional expectation E[Y | X], the best predictor of Y given X in mean square.
  • In statistics a conditional probability is a conditional relative frequency (row percentages of a contingency table), the basis of regression (E[Y | X = x]), of classification (P(class | features)) and of every comparison between groups. The same events give two different conditionals: P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A) in general (“confusion of the inverse”); the link between them is Bayes’ theorem.

Example: a diagnostic test

A disease has prevalence P(D) (the prior), the test has sensitivity P(+ | D) and specificity P(− | not D). What a patient wants to know is P(D | +), not the sensitivity.

2Bayes’ theorem and the three components

P(H | D) = P(D | H) P(H) / P(D),    P(D) = ∑i P(D | Hi) P(Hi) π(θ | x) = L(θ; x) π(θ) / ∫ L(θ; x) π(θ) dθ  ∝  L(θ; x) · π(θ) It follows from the multiplication rule written twice: P(H ∩ D) = P(D | H) P(H) = P(H | D) P(D).

  • The update. Multiply, then renormalise: values of θ that explain the data well and were already plausible gain weight; the others lose it. In odds form, posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio (Bayes factor): P(H | D)/P(Hc | D) = [P(H)/P(Hc)] · [P(D | H)/P(D | Hc)].
  • Why it is central. It is the only coherent way to combine existing information with new data, it answers the question people actually ask (the probability of a hypothesis or of a parameter value given the data, not of the data given a hypothesis), and the update is sequential: yesterday’s posterior is today’s prior, and processing the data in batches or all at once gives the same result.

Sequential updating: repeated positive tests

With the test of Section 1 (conditionally independent repetitions), each positive result multiplies the odds by LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity).

3Prior, likelihood and posterior for a proportion: the Beta–Binomial model

Unknown success probability θ (a conversion rate, a defect rate, a coin’s bias); data: x successes in n independent trials.

Likelihood: L(θ; x) = C(n, x) θx (1 − θ)n−x Prior: π(θ) ∝ θa−1 (1 − θ)b−1    (Beta(a, b), mean a/(a+b)) Posterior ∝ θx+a−1 (1 − θ)n−x+b−1  ⇒  Beta(a + x, b + n − x) The Beta prior is conjugate: the posterior is in the same family, and updating is just adding counts. a and b act as pseudo-observations (a successes, b failures) that were seen before the data. Posterior mean = (a + x)/(a + b + n) = w · [a/(a+b)] + (1 − w) · (x/n),   w = (a + b)/(a + b + n) a weighted average of the prior mean and the maximum-likelihood estimate: “shrinkage” towards the prior, strongest when n is small or the prior is concentrated.

4How much does the prior matter?

Same data, different priors

The posterior for the data of Section 3 under five priors, from vague to opinionated. With little data the conclusions differ; the likelihood is the same in all cases.

The prior washes out as data accumulate

Cromwell’s rule. A prior that gives probability 0 to a value can never be revised towards it, however much data arrive (0 × anything = 0). A dogmatic prior such as Beta(2, 30) (mass near 0.06) needs many observations to be overturned; a point mass never. Leave some prior probability on every plausible value.

5Prior and likelihood for a mean: the Normal–Normal model

Data x̄ from n observations N(θ, σ²) with σ known; prior θ ~ N(μ0, τ²). Working with precisions (1/variance), the posterior is normal with

1/τn² = 1/τ² + n/σ² ,    μn = (μ0/τ² + n x̄/σ²) · τn²Precisions add; the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean. With a flat prior (τ → ∞) it becomes x̄ ± σ/√n, the frequentist answer.

6Applications, advantages, limitations

AdvantagesLimitations
Uses prior information formally (earlier trials, physical constraints, expert knowledge) and reports how it changed.Prior specification is subjective; different reasonable priors can give different conclusions with small samples (Section 4). Sensitivity analysis is part of the job.
Direct statements about the parameter: “P(θ > 0.5 | data) = 0.94”, “95% credible interval”. This is what most users think a confidence interval or p-value means.Intervals and probabilities depend on the model and the prior; a bad model gives confident nonsense. Credible intervals do not in general have frequentist coverage guarantees.

Natural regularisation: small samples, rare events, many parameters (shrinkage; the MAP estimate with a Gaussian prior is ridge regression, with a Laplace prior the lasso).Improper or vague priors can behave badly (e.g. Bayes factors, the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox); a prior that excludes the truth is never corrected (Cromwell’s rule).
Hierarchical models, missing data, decision theory (minimising expected loss) fit naturally.Posterior probabilities of hypotheses are conditional on the model class: the true model may not be in it.

Typical applications

  • Diagnostic testing and screening (Section 1): the posterior depends on prevalence as much as on the quality of the test.
  • Spam filters / Naive Bayes classifiers: P(spam | words) ∝ P(spam) ∏ P(word | spam), assuming the words conditionally independent.
  • A/B tests and conversion rates (Beta–Binomial): P(rate B > rate A | data), expected loss, early stopping.
  • Estimation with prior knowledge (Normal–Normal): combining a measurement with a previous estimate: Kalman filter, meta-analysis, small-area estimation.
  • Forensics and law: the likelihood ratio of the evidence, and the “prosecutor’s fallacy” (P(evidence | innocent) mistaken for P(innocent | evidence)).

Frequentist versus Bayesian conclusions

  • Frequentist: θ is a fixed unknown, probability = long-run frequency of the procedure; a 95% confidence interval covers θ in 95% of repetitions, p-value = P(data at least this extreme | H0).
  • Bayesian: θ is described by a distribution of belief; a 95% credible interval has probability 0.95 of containing θ given the data and the prior; the output is P(H | data).
  • With a lot of data and a non-dogmatic prior the two agree numerically (Bernstein–von Mises theorem: the posterior is approximately N(θ̂MLE, 1/(n I(θ)))). They differ most for small samples, informative priors and hypothesis testing; compare the Wilson and credible intervals in Section 3.
Conclusion. Bayes’ theorem turns the likelihood, which says how probable the data are under each hypothesis, into the posterior, which says how probable each hypothesis is given the data, by weighting with the prior. The prior is both the strength and the weak point: it lets us use what we know, but it must be stated, justified and tested for sensitivity; with enough data the likelihood dominates and reasonable analysts agree.
